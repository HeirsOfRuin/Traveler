# Gate A: the read

The first of the two gates in `SPEC.md` ("Proof before screens"). Before any
work on this game's own rules, the author reads about 3,000 words and answers
three questions. Divine Mismanagement used the same gate. No instrument can
judge it.

**Status: written 2026-11-22, waiting on the author.**

## The three questions

1. Is it funny without trying?
2. Is there wonder?
3. Do you want the next scene?

A yes, a no, or "these parts" are all useful answers. If it is a no, there is
no tuning that fixes it (the SPEC says so), and the build stops here at the
cost of one evening.

## What was chosen to write it, and is not decided

The sample had to pick things the SPEC leaves open. None of these is a
decision until the author says so.

| Chosen for the sample | Why | Open? |
|---|---|---|
| Second person, present tense | Vikingr's voice, and the story engine's | Yes |
| The hero is a man who welds for a living and courts women | One setup had to be picked. A woman's arrival at the hall is a different scene, not this one with the pronouns changed, and is not written yet | The setup is the player's; the sample is one of them |
| "Now" is a machine shed two hours of gravel from a prairie town, unnamed | The door needed a workshop, and a long drive from the sea makes the smell of low tide do its work | Yes |
| The uncle is Walter; the hall hears "uncle" as his name, Unkel | He never told them another | Yes |
| The language conceit is a brass thing worn behind the ear, labelled "EAR. MOSTLY WORKS." | The SPEC asks for one conceit, stated once and left alone | Yes |
| The hall is Thorgrim Ketilsson's, a *hersir* (a local chieftain) on a western fjord. Aud Ketilsdóttir, his widowed sister, holds the keys. She is one of the two women | Invented people in a real place and year | Yes |
| The uncle left the hall six nights before the hero arrives | It puts the hero one step behind, as the SPEC asks. How time runs between the two sides of the door is not designed | Yes |

History in the sample: fire steels and tinder fungus, turf roofs, tarred
clinker boats, water and a towel offered to a guest (it is in *Hávamál*), and
a woman of the house carrying its keys are from memory of well-attested
things and are not yet in `docs/HISTORY.md`. The king is mentioned once and
not named, because where Harald's power lay is disputed.

The quoted lists are choices as the game would show them. The bold one is the
one the sample follows.

## 1. The shed

Your uncle Walter does not trust telephones, so the news comes by post. One
page, block capitals, the pen pressed hard enough to read from the back.

GOT IT WORKING. COME SATURDAY. BRING YOUR GOOD HELMET. DO NOT TELL YOUR MOTHER.

The postmark is nine days old. You know this because the letter sat on your
counter for six of them.

It is two hours of gravel to his place. The yard light is on at noon. His
truck is there, with the flat you told him about in the spring. The shed door
is rolled open a foot, the way he leaves it for the cat.

Inside, the heater is running and nobody is being heated. A cup of coffee on
the bench has grown a skin. The radio is on low, a man reading grain prices.

In the middle of the floor, bolted to the slab, is a door frame. No door and
no wall. Two-inch square tube, seven feet high, with cables as thick as your
wrist running from it to a rack of things you do not recognise and one thing
you do, which is your old stick welder, the one he said was stolen.

You look at the welds before you look at anything else. You cannot help it.
You taught him one summer and it did not take. Every joint on that frame is
his: cold, lumpy, and ground flat afterwards so nobody would know. Whatever
this thing does, those are what is holding it together.

On the bench, under a wrench, is half a sheet of paper.

*Gone through. Back for supper. If I am not, DO NOT FOLLOW. W.*

There is a thumbprint beside the W. It is brown, and it is not coffee.

Next to the note is a foam-lined case with two cut-outs. One is empty. The
other holds a brass thing the size of a hearing aid, with a hook to go behind
the ear. On the lid, on a strip of masking tape, in his capitals: EAR. MOSTLY
WORKS.

You stand in front of the frame. Through it you can see the back wall, the
pegboard, the calendar from the co-op, all where they should be. But the air
inside the frame is a shade darker than the air around it, the way a doorway
is, and there is a draught coming out of it that smells of woodsmoke and low
tide. The nearest tide is a three-day drive.

The cat is sitting against the far wall, as far from the frame as the shed
allows.

> - **Take the ear, fill your pockets from the bench, and go through.**
> - Go through now, before you think about it.
> - Drive back to where there is a signal and call someone.

*(What goes in the pockets is the hero's starting kit. The third choice is
honoured, and costs a day.)*

You hook the brass thing behind your ear. It is warm, which you did not expect
and do not like. Into your jacket go his lighter, a folding knife, a roll of
electrical tape and, after a moment, the note. Your phone is already in your
pocket. It has never occurred to you to leave it anywhere.

You step through the way you would step over an air hose.

## 2. The hill

You are standing in heather to the shin and it is raining sideways.

Behind you there is no frame, no shed and no slab. There is a boulder the size
of a grain truck with a streak of white quartz down it. You look at that
streak hard, because it is the only thing you have to come back to.

Then you turn around, and for a while you do nothing at all.

The hill drops away under your boots, grey rock and rust-coloured bracken,
down to water. The water is a long way below you and perfectly still,
black-green, and it runs inland between walls of mountain that go straight up
into the cloud. A waterfall comes down the far wall, thin and white. You
cannot hear it. It is that far.

There is not a wire in the sky. No road cut, no tower, no contrail, no hum.
You had not known there was a hum.

On a shelf of green land at the water's edge there is a building. It is
longer than your uncle's shed, low, and bowed along its length like a boat
turned over, and the roof is growing. There is grass on the roof. Smoke comes
off the ridge through no chimney you can see and lies flat in the rain.
Smaller roofs stand around it. There are strips of field, pale with stubble.
And on the shingle, drawn up side by side, are two boats, and even from here
the line of them is right: the long sweep up to the stem, like something
drawn in one stroke by a man who had drawn it all his life.

You have seen that line in a museum, behind glass, the planks black and
cracked and held up on steel. These are the colour of dark honey, and wet.
Somebody built them. He is probably down there now, out of the rain, and he
could build another.

You take out your phone. No service. Sixty-one per cent. You put it away.

It comes to you that you are very cold.

In the lee of the boulder you do what a person does. You gather sticks. You
have lit a thousand fires: with a torch, with a striker, with diesel when
nobody was looking. You hold your uncle's lighter under a handful of wet
heather and the heather steams at you. You try the bark off a little twisted
birch. You take out the note, look at it, and put it back. After a while the
lighter is hot enough to hurt your thumb, and you have made a small amount of
smoke and a smell.

"Hvat ertu?"

A boy is standing ten feet uphill of you with a dog and a stick. He is about
eleven. He is wearing wool the colour of the hillside, which is how he got so
close. The dog is doing the thinking for both of them, and it does not like
you.

The brass thing behind your ear clicks twice, like a relay.

"What are you?" the boy says again, and this time it is English, or it
arrives as English, half a beat behind his mouth.

Not who. What.

> - Tell him the truth: you are looking for your uncle.
> - **Tell him you are cold.**
> - Say nothing, and show him your empty hands.

"I'm cold," you say. Something happens in your throat on the way out that you
did not do. The boy hears it and frowns, the way you would frown at a man with
a thick accent, and then he looks at your sticks. He looks at the lighter. You
close your hand over it, too late.

He crouches. From the pouch at his belt he takes a curl of steel, a flake of
stone and a pinch of something black and felted. He puts his back to the wind
and one knee on your sticks, and he has a flame in less time than it took you
to find the lighter in your pocket. He feeds it bark from inside his shirt,
where it has been keeping dry against his skin. He does all of this without
looking at his hands. He is looking at your jacket.

"Can I?" he says, and does not wait. He finds the tab of the zip and pulls it
down, and up, and down. The teeth close behind it and open again. He does it
eleven times. You count, since there is nothing else you are able to
contribute.

"Unkel had one of these," he says. "On his trousers."

You hear your own voice go strange. "You know my uncle?"

"Everyone knows Unkel." He stands, and whistles the dog in. "He owes Thorgrim
a ship."

## 3. Unkel's water

The boy is called Kol. He goes down the hill like water and waits for you at
every turn without seeming to. You go down it like a man carrying a fridge.
Steel-toed boots, it turns out, were designed for a floor.

Then you are in the yard, with hens, and a woman who stops with a bucket in
each hand to watch you pass, and then you are through a door you have to duck
for, and inside.

It is dark, and it is enormous. A fire runs down the middle of the floor,
longer than a truck, and the light from it goes up and does not find the
roof. The posts that hold the roof are whole trees. Someone has carved them,
every one, from the floor to as high as the light goes: beasts biting beasts,
knotted so close you could not get a nail between them. It smells of smoke
and wet dog and meat and people. Thirty faces turn. The talk stops, a bench
at a time, from the door to the far end, and you hear the fire.

By the hearth a man is sitting with his sleeve pushed up and a cut across the
meat of his hand. An old woman holds his wrist. A girl is lifting a pot from
the fire with two sticks.

"Eighteen," the girl is saying. "Nineteen. Twenty. That is four."

"That is three," says the man with the hand.

"It is four, Bjorn. I have my thumb on it."

"Then your thumb moved."

They do it again, to be sure. The pot goes back. She counts to twenty. Only
then does the old woman pour, and the man hisses through his teeth, and she
binds the hand in a cloth that came out of the same pot.

"Unkel's water," Kol tells you, in the voice of a boy explaining something to
a much younger one. "You boil it for a hundred. Nobody would count a hundred
for a pot, so we count twenty, five times." He points with his chin at the
man. "Bjorn put an axe in his own leg last winter. The leg should have come
off. Unkel washed it with his water every day and shouted at anyone who
touched it, and Bjorn has the leg. So now it is done for everything. Cuts.
Lambs. Ulf washed his sword in it before he fought the man from Sogn."

"Did it help?"

"No. But nobody blames the water."

At the far end of the fire, in the one seat with a back to it, a man has been
watching all this without moving. He is about fifty and built like a door. He
crooks one finger. It is plain he has never had to crook it twice.

"You are Unkel's kin," Thorgrim says. It is not a question. "You have his
clothes and his way of standing in a room as if it should have told you it
was there." Someone laughs. "He ate at my fire from hay-time until the leaves
turned. He kept Bjorn's leg on. I liked him. He told me he could build a ship
that sails into the wind. Into it. I gave him half a pound of silver, to buy
iron. Six nights ago he walked up that hill with my silver, and he did not
walk down."

"What do you want with a ship that sails into the wind?"

"There is a king coming along this coast," Thorgrim says, "with the wind
behind him."

Nobody laughs at that.

"Tonight you are a guest," he says. "Tomorrow you are Unkel's kin, and what
Unkel owes. So. Can you build it?"

> - **Tell him you work iron. You have never built a ship.**
> - Tell him it can be built. You have seen boats that do it.
> - Tell him your uncle is a liar, and you are sorry for it.

*(This is the game's one decision, small. The second answer is true, raises
the hero fastest, and starts the hall wondering where he saw such boats.)*

## 4. The keys

"I work iron," you say. "I have never built a ship."

"Hm," says Thorgrim, to somebody behind you. "Is that true?"

You had not heard her come up. She is your age or near it, in a dress the
dark red of a rusted gate, with the sleeves pushed back like someone who has
been working and means to go on. At her belt is a ring of keys, a dozen at
least. You notice that when she came down the hall, people moved their feet.

She does not answer him. She takes your right hand and turns it over as she
would a fish she was thinking of buying.

She looks at the callus across the palm. She pushes your sleeve up, which
nobody has done to you since you were six, and looks at the forearm: the
shiny patches, the scatter of small white pits where spatter went through a
glove, the long one from the day you leaned on something you had just cut.
She runs her thumb over that one, once. Her hands are colder than yours and a
good deal harder.

"These are a smith's," she says.

"Yes."

"Unkel talked like a smith. He had hands like a bowl of curds." She lets go.
"So they sent us the mouth first and the hands after. I would have done it the
other way round." She says it over her shoulder to Thorgrim. "It is true. He
has been burned more than Hrapp has, and Hrapp is bad at it."

"I heard that," says somebody at the back.

"You were meant to."

This, Kol tells you, not quietly, is Aud. She is Thorgrim's sister. Her
husband went west with a ship three summers back and the ship came home
without him, and since then the keys are hers, and so is everything they
open.

A girl comes with a basin and a cloth and holds them out to you. You look at
the basin. You look at the girl. It has been a long day, and you are honestly
not sure whether you are meant to drink it. In the end you wipe your hands
down your jeans, to show willing.

Aud watches this from start to finish. Her face does not change at all, and
you would give the lighter and the knife together to know what that costs
her.

"Hands *in* the water," she says. "Then the cloth. Then you eat. Unkel knew
that much, and Unkel once tried to milk the ram."

You wash. She points you to a place on the bench by the door, among the dogs.
That is where a man sits until the hall knows what he is. A bowl comes, of
something grey and hot with barley in it. No spoon comes. You look around.
Every person in the hall has taken a spoon of their own from a belt or a
sleeve. You have a roll of electrical tape.

She has seen that too. She takes a spoon of pale horn from her belt, worn
thin at the lip, and holds it out, and does not let go of it when you take
the other end.

"I have had this longer than I had my husband," she says. "It comes back."

"It comes back."

She lets go. Then, low, so it stays under the noise of the hall: "He was
bleeding when he went up the hill. His hand, wrapped in his shirt. He would
not say who. Did he come home to you bleeding?"

The note is in your jacket. A thumbprint, brown, beside the W.

So he did get home. He stood at his own bench with his hand wrapped in his
shirt, and wrote that he would be back for supper, and went through the door
again. And he did not come here.

> - **Show her the note.**
> - Tell her he got home safe.
> - Ask her whose knife it was.
