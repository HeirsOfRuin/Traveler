# Gate A: the read

The first of the two gates in `SPEC.md`. The author reads a sample and says
whether it is funny without trying, whether there is wonder, and whether he
wants the next scene. No instrument can judge it.

**The living text is now `content/scenes/`** (the first prototype, 2026-11-24).
It has had a pass for readability since this draft, and its letters and notes
are drawn. This file is the draft as it was read; where the two differ, the
scenes win.

**Status: fourth draft, 2026-11-23.** The third draft
was accepted ("Gate a is fine for now, call it usable for the prototype").
This one replaces it at his word: "Rewrite gate a with all of the new context
behind it". The first three drafts are in this file's git history.

## What this draft is built on

Everything decided since the third draft was written, each in the author's
words in the SPEC's table:

| Decision | What it does to the sample |
|---|---|
| The game is using what you know without being taken for a lunatic | The hall's choice is no longer "can you build it". The hero does a modern thing in front of everybody, and the choice is **how to present it**: as a craft, done and left unexplained, or explained |
| A hero is an occupation, two traits, a look and a manner | The sample is written for every hero. Where the text depends on who was made, each version is written and labelled |
| Looks and manner change the prose more than the outcome | They change a line here and there, and no road |
| Every choice is three roads of the same story, none of them a refusal | Two choices, and all three answers to each are written. Nothing is in bold |
| Not enough dry wit; keep the machine feel out | The narrator reports, drily. No short line alone at the end of a paragraph for effect, no telling the reader what they have just worked out, no lists of three for rhythm, no echoed closing line, no joke that knows it is one |
| The door stays shut four years | Not in the sample. It is the wait the visits lead to |

## How to read it

Plain text is what every hero reads. A labelled block is one version of a
passage; a player sees one of them and never knows there were others.

- **By occupation** (welder, nurse, history teacher, engineer): what the hero
  looks at first in the shed, what is in the work bag, and how much of a
  hurry they are in when a man stops breathing.
- **By trait**: two of the eight are shown, once each. Every trait gets
  passages like these through the game.
- **By look and by manner**: three small passages, to show the size of them.
- **By sex**: one passage, where the hall seats a stranger. A woman's time in
  the hall will differ by much more than a bench; that is still to be
  written, and this draft does not pretend otherwise.
- **By choice**: the three roads after each of the two choices.

History in the sample, from memory and not yet in `docs/HISTORY.md`: fire
steels and tinder fungus, turf roofs, tarred clinker boats, water and a towel
offered to a guest (*Hávamál*), a woman of the house carrying its keys, a
women's cross-bench in a hall, everybody bringing their own spoon. Sugar was
not known in Norway in 870 and pepper was rare. The king is mentioned once
and not named, because where Harald's power lay is disputed.

## 1. The shed

Your uncle Walter does not trust telephones, so the news comes by post, on one
page, in block capitals pressed hard enough to read from the back.

GOT IT WORKING. COME SATURDAY. BRING WHAT YOU USE AT WORK. DO NOT TELL YOUR
MOTHER.

He has ended every letter that way since you were nine, including the
Christmas cards, which he addresses to her. He did not say which Saturday. You
let that carry you past the first one, and it is the second when you go.

It is two hours of gravel to his place. The yard light is on at noon and his
truck is in the yard with the flat you mentioned in April, when he said he was
aware of it. The shed door is rolled open a foot for the cat.

Inside, the heater is running for nobody. There is a cup of coffee on the
bench with a skin on it, and the radio is on low, where a man is reading out
the price of canola.

Bolted to the slab in the middle of the floor is a door frame, with no door in
it and no wall around it. It is seven feet of two-inch square tube, and cables
as thick as your wrist run from it to a rack of equipment you cannot name,
apart from one item, which is the arc welder that went missing from your
mother's garage four years ago. He was very decent about it at the time.

> **A welder.** You look at the welds first, because you always do. You gave
> him a summer of lessons once and he gave it back. He has ground every joint
> flat and painted it, and you know from experience what a person is hiding
> when they go to that trouble on a frame nobody will see. Whatever this one
> does, it does on the strength of those.

> **A nurse.** You look at the bench first, because eleven years have taught
> you to find the mess before you look at the patient. There is a roll of
> gauze with its wrapper torn open by teeth, a bottle of the brown antiseptic
> nobody has stocked since you trained, and a tea towel that will not be used
> for tea again. Whatever he cut, he dressed it one-handed and in a hurry, and
> about as well as he does most things.

> **A history teacher.** You look at the shelf first, because you read
> people's shelves. There is the Old Norse grammar you lent him two
> Christmases ago and wrote off, with its spine broken at the verbs. Next to
> it are a road atlas of Italy and a paperback history of Japan with a bus
> ticket a third of the way in. He has never been known to finish a book, and
> you would have said he had never been known to start three.

> **An engineer.** You look at the supply first, because somebody ought to.
> The cables run back to the panel by the door, where he has pulled the
> breaker for the grain dryer and fitted something of his own made out of
> copper bar. There is a scorch on the wall above it in the shape of a hand.
> Nothing in this building is rated for whatever goes through that frame, and
> the building has plainly told him so.

Under a wrench on the bench is half a sheet of paper.

*Gone through. Back for supper. If I am not, DO NOT FOLLOW. W.*

He has underlined NOT twice. Beside the W is a thumbprint in something brown,
which has dried as blood dries and coffee does not.

Beside the note is a foam-lined case cut for two of something. One place is
empty. In the other is a brass object about the size of a hearing aid, with a
hook to sit behind the ear, and a strip of masking tape on the lid reads EAR.
MOSTLY WORKS. The truck has a label like that on the dash.

Through the frame you can see the back wall of the shed, the pegboard and the
co-op calendar, all where they belong. The air inside it is a little darker
than the air outside it, though, as it is in any doorway, and a draught is
coming through that smells of woodsmoke and low tide. The nearest tide is
three days' drive from here.

The cat has taken the far wall, and from the state of the blanket it has had
the far wall for some time.

There is more tape on the frame itself, at eye height. ONE HAND'S WORTH. ASK
ME HOW I KNOW.

So you are going, it seems, and the question is what the hand is for. Your
work bag is in the truck, since he asked for it. His notebook is on the bench
beside the note, a black one gone fat with use, with a rubber band round it.
And over the kettle is a shelf he has labelled TRADE, which holds a bag of
sugar, a tin of pepper and three cards of steel needles from the fabric shop
in town. You had taken that for groceries and a hobby.

> - Fetch your work bag from the truck.
> - Take his notebook.
> - Empty the TRADE shelf into a feed sack.

*(Three ways to be a stranger: with your trade, with what he knew, or with
something to give. Each is written below.)*

You hook the brass thing behind your ear and find it warm, as if it had lately
been behind somebody else's. The note goes into your jacket, since a person
should have something to show the police. Your phone and your lighter are in
your pockets already, where they live.

You step through the frame as you would over a garden hose, without looking
down, with your one hand's worth.

## 2. The hill

You are up to the shins in heather and the rain is coming in from the side.

The frame has not come with you, nor the shed. Behind you is a boulder about
the size of a grain truck, with a streak of white quartz down the face of it,
and you take a good look at that streak, since it is all the address you have.

Then you turn around, and it is some time before you do anything else.

The hill falls away below your boots in grey rock and rusty bracken, down to
water that lies a long way under you, black-green and so still that it holds
the mountains upside down. It runs inland between walls of rock that go up
into the cloud and do not come out again. Across from you a waterfall hangs on
the far wall, a white thread of it, too far off to hear.

There is no wire anywhere in the sky and no road cut into the other shore, and
it is quieter than you knew a place could be.

On a shelf of green by the water stands a building, longer than your uncle's
shed and much lower, bowed along its length like the hull of a boat turned
over. Grass is growing on the roof. Smoke leaks out along the ridge, having
found no chimney, and lies down flat in the rain. Smaller roofs stand about
it, and strips of field gone pale with stubble, and on the shingle below are
two boats drawn up side by side. Even from this height you know the line of
them, the long rise to the stem that somebody drew in one stroke.

> **Sails.** The wind is coming up the water from the sea, and the smoke
> agrees with you. Both boats have been hauled out with their sterns to it,
> by somebody who did not have to look either.

The only other one you have seen was in a museum, black and cracked, propped
on steel behind glass. These two are the colour of dark honey and they are
wet. Whoever built them is very likely down there under the grass roof,
keeping dry, and could build you a third if you had anything to pay him with.

You try your phone, which has no service and sixty-one per cent, and put it
away.

By now you are cold enough to notice. You get into the lee of the boulder and
collect sticks, with some confidence, having lit fires before, in a pit, on a
dry evening, with a firelighter. Wet heather held over the lighter gives off
steam. The bark of a small bent birch does no more. You take out the note and
consider it, and decide your uncle would want it kept. By the time the lighter
is too hot to hold you have produced a smell.

"Hvat ertu?"

A boy is standing a few yards up the hill with a dog and a stick. He might be
eleven. His wool is the colour of the hillside, which accounts for how near he
has got, and the dog beside him has plainly reached a decision about you and
is waiting for him to catch up.

The brass thing clicks twice behind your ear, like a relay closing.

"What are you?" he says again. This time it reaches you in English, half a
beat after his mouth has finished. He has asked what, you notice, and not who.

> **Blunt.** "Cold," you tell him.

> **Quiet.** You hold up your hands, which have gone the colour of the
> boulder, and let him work it out.

> **Cheerful.** "Cold, thanks," you tell him, as you might to somebody at a
> bus stop.

> **Stubborn.** "Lighting a fire," you tell him, over the place where there
> is no fire.

Your throat does something to the words on their way out that you had no part
in, and he frowns as people do at a heavy accent. Then he looks at your
sticks, and at the lighter, which you are slow to cover.

He crouches down. Out of the pouch on his belt come a curl of steel, a flake
of stone and a pinch of black felted stuff, and with his back to the wind and
a knee on your sticks he has a flame going before you have the lighter back in
your pocket. He feeds it with bark he has been carrying inside his shirt,
where it keeps dry. His hands manage all this alone, because the rest of him
is studying your jacket.

"Can I?" he says, and has hold of the zip before you answer. He runs it down
and up and watches the teeth shut behind the tab, and does it again, eleven
times in all. You keep count, there being nothing else for you to do at your
own fire.

"Unkel had one of these," he says. "On his trousers."

"You know my uncle?"

"Everyone knows Unkel. He owes Thorgrim a ship."

After that he gets round to what you are carrying.

### If you brought your work bag

He has it open before you can decide whether to stop him.

> **A welder.** The helmet he puts on, and takes off again directly, having
> found that it makes the world dark. The gloves he approves of. Then he
> comes to the file. He tries its edge with his thumb and draws it once along
> the back of his fire-steel, where it lifts a bright curl and leaves a flat,
> and he looks at the flat for some time.
>
> "You are a smith," he says. "Hrapp is our smith." He puts the file back
> with more care than he took it out. "Hrapp is going to hate you."

> **A nurse.** The scissors he approves of. The gloves he blows up, ties off
> and gives to the dog, which does not want one. Then he finds the
> stethoscope, and after some thought puts the wrong end in his mouth. You
> fit it to his ears and hold the other end against his chest. He listens to
> himself for a while, and afterwards to the dog, and you can see him
> deciding which of them is the better made.
>
> "You are a healer," he says. "Gyda is our healer." He hands the thing back
> by its tubing, as he would a live eel. "Gyda is going to hate you."

> **A history teacher.** It holds thirty-one essays on the causes of the
> First World War, which you had meant to mark at your uncle's kitchen table.
> He turns over a good many pages before he sees that the marks on them are
> all different, and that somebody made every one. The red pen he clicks
> until you take it from him. He draws a line with it across the back of his
> hand and holds the hand out to the rain to see whether it will stay.
>
> "You make marks for remembering," he says. "Ulf remembers for us, and he
> needs no marks." He gives you back the essays, in no order. "Ulf is going
> to hate you."

> **An engineer.** The hard hat he puts on and keeps on. The calculator he
> shakes, in case there is something in it. Then he finds the tape, draws out
> an arm's length of it, and lets go. When it has finished coming back at him
> he does it again, and then he measures the dog, which is forty-one inches
> from nose to tail and holds still for it.
>
> "You measure things," he says. "Orm builds our ships and he measures
> nothing. He looks." He winds the tape in by hand, slowly, to see where it
> goes. "Orm is going to hate you."

He appears to be looking forward to it. He says he is called Kol, and he
carries the bag down the hill himself, shouting before the yard is in earshot
that he has found something on the mountain and that it was he who found it.

### If you brought the notebook

It is open on your knee, where you had been hunting for a page about fire. The
page you have instead is headed PEOPLE, and the third line of it reads: *KOL.
Boy, about 11. Dog. Finds everything first, tells everyone.*

"You're Kol," you say.

The boy stops with his hand on the dog's neck. "Who gave you my name?"

You have no answer he would like, so you hold up the book. He looks at the
marks on the page as he might at the tracks of an animal he had only been told
about, and he does not come any nearer to them. Further down it says
*THORGRIM. Runs the place. Fair.* Under that is *AUD. His sister. Do not bother
lying to her.* At the foot, in a box drawn twice round: *STOP EXPLAINING THINGS.* He
has underlined that as well, and from the look of the paper he did it on
several different days.

Kol goes down the hill well ahead of you and does not wait at the turns. When
you reach the yard on your own, a good while after him, a number of people
have found work to do near the door, and it is clear that the news has arrived
and that it was about the names.

### If you brought the feed sack

He looks into it and is not much struck. You tear a corner off the sugar and
shake some into his palm, and he takes it for salt and licks it up out of
manners.

He stands quite still. Then he cleans the palm thoroughly, looks into the sack
to see how much more of it there is, and ties the neck himself, with a knot
you would not care to have to undo.

"Do not give that to anybody on the way down," he says. "They will only want
it."

He lets you know that he is called Kol and that you may walk behind him. In
the yard he informs a woman carrying buckets, and after her the hens, that the
stranger is his, that he found it, and that nobody is to touch the sack.

## 3. Unkel's water

Your boots were bought for a floor. Nobody sold them to you for wet moss, and
you make most of the descent sitting.

> **Rode as a child.** There is a horse in the yard, about the size of a
> large pony and the colour of the hill. It looks you over as the ones at the
> riding school used to, and you can tell that it has come to the same view.

The door of the long building is cut for people who know where the lintel is.

> **Tall.** You find out where it is.

> **Broad.** You go through it at an angle, as a sofa would.

> **Slight.** You pass under it with room to spare, and it is the last thing
> you do all day as well as the people who live here.

Inside it is dark and far bigger than it looked from the hill. A fire burns in
a trench down the middle of the floor, a truck-length of it, and its light
climbs the posts and gives up before it reaches the roof. The posts are whole
trees. Somebody has carved every one of them, from the floor to as high as you
can see, with beasts that bite each other and are knotted so tight you could
not get a nail in between. The place smells of smoke and wet dog, of meat and
of a good many people, some thirty of whom have turned to look at you. The
talk stops one bench at a time, working up from the door to the far end, until
the fire is the loudest thing in the room.

> **Easy to forget.** *(In place of the last two sentences.)* The place
> smells of smoke and wet dog, of meat and of a good many people, none of
> whom has turned round. You stand inside the door for long enough to learn
> the price of a ewe. In the end it is a small child who points.

Near the hearth sits a man with his sleeve pushed up and a cut across the heel
of his hand. An old woman has him by the wrist, and a girl is lifting a pot
off the fire between two sticks.

"Eighteen," the girl is saying. "Nineteen. Twenty. That is four."

"That is three," says the man with the hand.

"It is four, Bjorn. I have my thumb on it."

"Then your thumb moved."

They count it again to settle the matter. Only when the girl has reached
twenty does the old woman pour, and the man lets his breath out through his
teeth, and she ties up the hand in a cloth out of the same pot.

"Unkel's water," Kol says, in the tone of a boy with a slow pupil. "You boil
it for a hundred. Nobody is going to count a hundred for a pot, so we count
twenty, five times." He lifts his chin at the man. "Bjorn put an axe in his
own leg last winter and it should have come off. Unkel washed it in his water
every day and shouted at anybody who touched it, and Bjorn still has the leg."

"Did he say why it works?"

"He said there are beasts in water, too small to see, and the boiling kills
them." Kol considers you, to find out whether you are the kind of person who
would believe that. "Nobody listened to that part. But the leg stayed on. So
we boil it, and we leave out the beasts."

At the head of the fire, in the only seat with a back, a man of about fifty
has watched your arrival without moving anything but his eyes. He is built
along the same lines as the posts. He lifts one finger, and you go up the
hall.

*(His first words depend on what was carried.)*

- **The work bag.** "Kol tells me he has found something useful," Thorgrim
  says. "The last one off that hill told me he was useful as well."
- **The notebook.** "Kol tells me you had his name before he gave it,"
  Thorgrim says. "Have you mine?" You have, and you say it, and the people on
  the bench nearest you discover that there is room further along.
- **The feed sack.** You put the pepper and a card of needles on the board in
  front of him. He opens the tin and sneezes, and is pleased with it. "Unkel
  came with his hands empty and went away with them full," Thorgrim says. "You
  have it the other way about."

"You are his kin, though. You have his clothes, and you stand in my hall as he
did, as if somebody were about to explain it to you." There is some laughing
at this. "He ate at my fire from hay-time until the leaves turned, and he ate
well. He kept Bjorn's leg on, and reminded us of it most evenings. I liked
him. He said he could build me a ship that would sail into the wind. I asked
how, and he drew it in the ashes, and it was a handsome drawing. I gave him
half a pound of silver to buy iron. Six nights ago he went up that hill with
it and has not come down."

"What do you want with a ship that sails into the wind?"

"There is a king working his way along this coast," Thorgrim says, "and he
has the wind behind him. Tonight you are my guest. Tomorrow you are Unkel's
kin, and you have what Unkel owes. We will talk about my ship when you have
eaten."

> **A man.** You are given the end of the bench nearest the door, among the
> dogs, where a stranger sits until the hall has made up its mind.

> **A woman.** You are given the end of the cross-bench at the far wall,
> where the women of the house sit, and where you are gone over a good deal
> more thoroughly than Thorgrim troubled to.

A girl brings you a basin of water and a cloth and holds them out. You look at
the basin and then at the girl. It has been a long day, and you cannot
honestly say whether the water is for drinking. You settle it by wiping your
hands down your jeans, to show you mean well.

A woman has stopped behind the girl to watch this. She is about your age, in a
dress of the dark red that a gate goes when it has rusted properly, with the
sleeves shoved back from work she has not finished, and at her belt hangs a
ring of keys, a dozen or more. People along the benches drew in their feet as
she came. Her face, through the whole business with the basin, stays exactly
where it was, and you suspect that took some doing.

"Hands in the water," she says. "After that the cloth, and after that you eat.
Unkel had it by his second month."

A bowl arrives with something grey and hot in it, and barley in that. No spoon
arrives. Along both benches people are taking spoons out of belts and sleeves,
having brought their own, as people evidently do. She unhooks one of pale horn
from beside the keys, worn thin along one edge, and holds it out, and keeps
hold of her end when you take yours.

"I have had this longer than I had my husband," she says. "I want it back."

Kol informs you, at no great pains to be quiet, that this is Aud, Thorgrim's
sister, and that the keys are hers and so is whatever they open.

## 4. The smith's throat

Across the fire a man with burned forearms has been telling his end of the
bench about Unkel, and doing the voice. He does it well enough that you know
it for your uncle through a brass ear and eleven hundred years. Halfway
through a sentence about the ship he stops.

Nobody minds at first. He has a mouthful, and a man with a mouthful is allowed
to stop. Then he gets to his feet, and puts a hand flat on the board, and the
other to his throat, and makes no sound at all. The man next to him laughs and
thumps him between the shoulders. The smith's face has gone a colour you have
seen on a plum.

> **A nurse.** You have done this eleven times, twice on the same patient,
> and you are over the bench and round the fire before you have decided to
> be.

> **Anybody else.** You once spent a wet afternoon in a church hall being
> shown this on a rubber torso by a man called Keith. You find, to your
> surprise, that it took.

You get behind him, put your arms round his middle, make a fist under his ribs
with the other hand over it, and pull.

It is only now, with your arms round a stranger from behind in a hall full of
his kin, that you see how it looks. Three men are on their feet. One of them
has a knife out, the short one he was eating with, and is coming round the end
of the bench with it. You pull again, and something leaves the smith at speed
and lands in the fire, where it hisses.

He breathes in, with a noise like a door being forced. Then he calls you
something the brass ear declines to translate.

Nobody in the hall is talking. The man with the knife has stopped, though he
has not put it away.

"What," says Thorgrim, "did you do to my smith?"

> - Tell him it is a hold your people use for a bone in the throat.
> - Tell him the man was choking and now he is not, and sit down.
> - Tell him how it works.

*(This is the game's standing decision, at its smallest. The thing is done
either way; a hero uses what they know. The choice is how it is presented: as
a craft, done and left alone, or explained. Each is written below, and each is
a different first conversation with Aud.)*

### If you call it a hold

"It is a hold," you say. "Where I come from it is taught for a bone in the
throat. The fist goes here, under the ribs, and you pull up and in."

Thorgrim nods slowly. A hold is a thing a hall understands. They wrestle here
all winter, having little else to do, and the man with the knife puts it away
and asks to be shown where the fist goes.

You show it on Kol, who has volunteered by standing closest. Before the bowls
are empty there are three pairs of boys by the door doing it to one another,
and one of them has been sick. The old woman who poured the water comes down
the hall to ask whether there is a word that is said with it. You tell her
there is not. She is disappointed in you, and you gather from Kol that there
will be a word by tomorrow whether you supply one or not.

Aud sits down on the bench beside you, which makes the bench go quiet.

"'Where I come from it is taught,'" she says. "That was well put. Unkel would
have told us why it works, and Hrapp would have died of listening."

"It is taught, where I come from."

"I do not doubt it." She takes back her spoon, looks it over, and hangs it by
the keys. "Hrapp will not thank you. He hates to owe, and he liked Unkel less
than anybody here did. I would stay out of the forge for a day or two."

### If you say nothing more

"He was choking," you say. "Now he isn't."

You step back over the bench, sit down and pick up your bowl, which is still
warm.

For a little while nothing happens. After that the hall does your explaining
for you, at some length, and you eat through it. By the time you reach the
bottom of the barley you have been a wrestler from the east, and you have been
one of Unkel's people, who are known to have healing in their hands. The old
woman who poured the water holds that you squeezed a thing out of Hrapp that
had been in him since midwinter, and that he is better without it. Nobody asks
you which. Thorgrim has you moved four places up the bench, into the warm, and
somebody you have not met fills your cup.

Aud comes for her spoon when you have finished with it.

"You let them tell it," she says. "Unkel never once managed that. He would
have been on his feet by now, drawing in the ashes."

"There wasn't much to tell."

"No." She turns your hand over, looks at the palm as if it might say more than
you had, and gives it back. "I will want to hear it, all the same. It need not
be tonight."

### If you explain

"There are two pipes in the throat," you say. "One is for air and the other is
for food. The meat went down the one for air. There was still air in his
chest, under the meat, so I squeezed the chest, and the air pushed it out."

Bjorn feels his own neck with his good hand. The old woman who poured the
water asks, quite reasonably, why in that case a man cannot breathe and
swallow together all day long. You tell her there is a flap that closes.

"A door," says someone further down. "In the throat."

"A small one."

You hear it, then, as they are hearing it. Thorgrim looks at you for a time
without unkindness.

"Unkel talked like this," he says. "The beasts in the water."

Hrapp, who owes you his life, has moved a place further down the bench from
you. Several people have found something to look at in their bowls. Aud takes
you by the sleeve, walks you the length of the hall to the door post, and
stands with her back to the room.

"Was it true? About the pipes."

"Yes."

"I thought it might be. Unkel's were mostly true as well." She keeps her voice
under the noise, which is starting up again behind her. "They will have a true
thing out of your hands any day of the year. Out of your mouth it is only
talk. The last one here who talked like that went up the hill six nights ago
with his hand wrapped in his shirt."

"Whose knife was it?"

"Eat your barley," Aud says. "And I will have my spoon back when you have."

## Where each road leaves the hero

Nothing here is a system yet; it is what the prose above already did.

| What was carried | The hall thinks you are | You have | You do not have |
|---|---|---|---|
| Work bag | Something useful, and a problem for whoever does that work here | A trade on the first night, and somebody who hates you | Anything to give, or any idea who these people are |
| Notebook | Something that knows names | The uncle's own account of the place, and his advice | The hall's ease. They moved along the bench |
| Feed sack | Kol's find, and a guest who gives | Thorgrim's good humour and a boy who guards you | A use, once the sack is empty |

| How it was presented | The hall | Aud | The uncle's trail |
|---|---|---|---|
| As a hold | Has a new hold, and will have a word for it by morning. You keep your bench | Has seen you dress a thing up, and approves | Hrapp disliked him more than anyone did |
| Left alone | Has explained you to itself three different ways. Four places up, in the warm | Has a question, and will come back to it | Nothing. She did not say |
| Explained | Has heard that a man has a door in his throat. One place of room on either side of you | Has told you how this hall takes a true thing, and that it is the same mistake he made | He was bleeding when he left, and somebody here did it |

The third road is the one the game warns against, and it is the only one that
tells the hero what happened to the uncle. That is on purpose: the explained
road should cost standing and buy something, or nobody would ever take it
twice.
